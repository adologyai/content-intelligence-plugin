# Copywriting Guide for the Creative Toolkit

This guide governs how every piece of copy in the toolkit gets written. Read this before
generating any hooks, taglines, copy lines, concepts, or scripts.

## The Core Problem You're Solving

AI-generated copy has a recognizable voice. Creative teams spot it instantly and throw it away.
The toolkit becomes worthless if it reads like "a robot brainstormed this." Your job is to
produce copy that a senior copywriter would look at and think "that's a good line" — not
"that's clearly AI."

## The AI Slop Blacklist

These words and patterns are banned from the toolkit. They're statistically overused by AI
(300%+ above human baseline) and trigger immediate "this is AI" recognition:

**Banned verbs:** leverage, empower, elevate, delve, dive into, foster, unleash, unlock,
ignite, streamline, uncover, underscore, optimize, navigate, spearhead, bolster

**Banned adjectives:** cutting-edge, seamless, robust, future-ready, multifaceted, pivotal,
dynamic, comprehensive, game-changing, transformative, revolutionary, best-in-class

**Banned phrases:** "In today's [anything]...", "In the ever-evolving landscape of...",
"Let's dive in", "Here's the kicker", "It is important to note", "It could be argued",
"At its core", "Imagine a world where", "At the end of the day", "That being said"

**Banned metaphors:** tapestry, landscape (as metaphor), beacon, symphony, ecosystem
(unless literal), journey (unless literal customer journey), roadmap (unless literal)

**Banned openers for copy lines:** "Discover the...", "Unlock your...", "Elevate your...",
"Transform your...", "Experience the..."

If you catch yourself writing any of these, stop and rewrite using plain, specific language.

## How Good Copy Actually Works

### 1. Specificity Over Everything

The single biggest difference between AI copy and human copy is specificity. AI defaults to
abstract claims. Humans write about concrete details.

BAD: "Our product delivers exceptional results"
GOOD: "47 seconds from box to first use. No app download required."

BAD: "High-quality protein for active lifestyles"
GOOD: "20g protein. 4 ingredients. Fits in your jacket pocket."

BAD: "Transform your skincare routine"
GOOD: "The redness was gone by Thursday."

Every copy line in the toolkit should contain at least one specific detail — a number, a time,
a name, a sensory detail, a concrete outcome. If a line is all adjectives and no specifics,
it's not ready.

### 2. Write Like a Human Talks

Read every line aloud. If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it. If it sounds like
something you'd text a friend about a product you actually love, it's close.

The pub test: Would you say this to someone at a bar? "This energy drink seamlessly integrates
into your active lifestyle" — no. "This stuff doesn't make me jittery and it actually tastes
good" — yes.

Use contractions. Start sentences with "And" or "But." Use fragments. Break grammar rules
when it creates rhythm. Real people don't speak in complete, perfectly structured sentences.

### 3. Rhythm Is Meaning

Vary sentence length deliberately. AI writes in monotonous 15-18 word sentences.
Good copy alternates:

Long sentence that builds context and paints a picture of what the audience is experiencing
right now, the frustration they feel, the gap between where they are and where they want to be.
Then short. Like this. Three words. Then back to medium length for the solution.

The short sentence after the long one is where the impact lives. That's where the hook lands,
the claim sticks, the CTA converts.

### 4. One Idea Per Line

Every copy line in the toolkit should carry exactly one idea. Not a compound sentence with
three benefits. One clear, sharp thought.

BAD: "Our bars are high in protein, low in sugar, and made with clean ingredients for
people who care about what they put in their bodies"
GOOD: "20g protein. 2g sugar. You can read every ingredient."

Three lines that each land one punch > one line that tries to land three.

### 5. Benefits Over Features (But Make Them Specific)

Features tell what it is. Benefits tell what it does for you. But generic benefits are as
useless as features. The best copy makes the benefit specific and sensory.

Feature: "Contains 200mg caffeine"
Generic benefit: "Gives you energy"
Specific benefit: "You'll still be sharp at 4pm when everyone else hits the wall"

Feature: "Clean-label ingredients"
Generic benefit: "Better for your health"
Specific benefit: "Your nutritionist won't side-eye you for eating it"

### 6. Tension Before Resolution

Every good piece of copy creates a small tension — a problem, a question, a gap — before
resolving it. This is the engine of PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solve) and it works at every scale:

In a hook: "Your morning bar has 14 grams of sugar. Mine has 2." (tension: you're eating
sugar you didn't know about. resolution: there's a better option.)

In a tagline: "Real food. No apology." (tension: most "healthy" brands apologize for taste
or texture. resolution: this one doesn't.)

In a script: Open with the problem (meal prep takes hours), agitate it (you spend Sunday
cooking instead of living), solve it (one bar, 30 seconds, done).

### 7. The "Delete the First Line" Rule

AI almost always warms up with a throat-clearing sentence before getting to the actual copy.
"When it comes to protein bars, there are many options on the market." Delete that. The real
hook is the second sentence.

After writing any copy, delete the first sentence and see if it's better. It almost always is.

## Frameworks to Use

When generating copy for the toolkit, use these structures:

**For hooks:** PAS micro-format — problem in 5 words, agitate with a specific detail, solve
with the product in one line.

**For captions:** BAB — paint the before-state (relatable struggle), show the after-state
(specific outcome), bridge with the product.

**For CTAs:** Be direct and specific. Not "Learn more" but "Comment FUEL and I'll send you
the ingredient list." Not "Shop now" but "Try one for $2."

**For taglines:** Find the tension the brand lives in and name it in 3-6 words. "Real food.
No apology." "Energy that doesn't lie." "Made for Monday mornings."

**For scripts:** Open mid-action (not with setup). The viewer should feel like they walked
into a conversation already happening. Use the UGC structure: problem ID (0-3s) → personal
story (4-10s) → product in use (11-20s) → specific result (21-28s) → soft CTA (last 2s).

## Voice Adaptation

Every line must sound like the brand, not like a copywriter. After extracting the Voice DNA:

- Check every line against the preferred/avoided vocabulary lists
- Match the tone dimension positions (if the brand is "leans-casual," don't write formal copy)
- Use the brand's sentence structure preference (short-punchy vs. balanced vs. flowing)
- Match emoji and contraction patterns
- Write in the brand's person preference (first/second/third)

The voice check: read the line and imagine it coming from the brand's social media manager.
If it sounds like it could come from any brand, it's not voiced enough. If you could swap in
a competitor's name and it still works, rewrite it with more specificity.

## Volume With Quality

The toolkit needs volume (100-150+ items) but every item must meet the quality bar. Don't
pad with generic lines. If you can't make a specific, voiced, surprising version of a line,
cut it and replace it with something better.

Better to have 80 lines that are all usable than 150 where 70 are filler.

## The Editor Pass

After generating the full toolkit, switch hats from writer to editor. These are different
modes — the writer generates range and volume, the editor cuts and tightens. Don't try to do
both at once; the editor pass happens AFTER all sections are drafted.

### What Editors Actually Do

A good editor isn't polishing sentences. They're asking: does this earn its place? Specifically:

**Killing darlings.** A line can be well-written and still not belong. If it's clever but
doesn't serve the campaign, cut it. If it's a good line for a different brand, cut it. The
toolkit isn't a portfolio of your best writing — it's ammunition for a specific team working
on a specific brief.

**Catching reaches.** When data shows "2.1x engagement on UGC hooks in this category" and
the copy says "the #1 hook format dominating social media," that's a reach. The editor pulls
claims back to what the data actually showed. Specificity applies to proof claims too, not
just product claims.

**Removing the nonsensical.** Pattern-matching sometimes produces items that parse grammatically
but don't mean anything real. "Hydration that speaks your language" — what does that actually
mean? If you can't explain the literal meaning in plain English, the line is nonsense dressed
up as copy. Cut it.

**Tightening.** Most first-draft lines are 20-30% longer than they need to be. The editor
finds the two words doing one word's job and picks the better one. "In the morning when you
first wake up" → "Every morning." This isn't about making things shorter for brevity's sake —
it's about making every word earn its place.

**Consistency.** After editing 100+ items, check that the voice hasn't drifted. Read 10 items
in sequence from different sections. Do they sound like the same brand? Editing tends to
flatten voice toward "clean and professional" — the final pass puts the brand's fingerprints
back on anything that got over-smoothed.
