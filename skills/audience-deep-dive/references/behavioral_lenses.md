# Behavioral lenses — working toolkit

Use this when writing the **System 1 decoder** in step 8 of the workflow. The goal is to name the heuristic or bias actually driving behavior, not just describe the behavior.

## Core dual-process framing (Kahneman)

- **System 1** — fast, automatic, emotional, pattern-matching. Most consumer decisions live here, even ones people *think* are rational.
- **System 2** — slow, deliberate, effortful. This is where stated survey answers come from. Don't confuse what people write on a survey with what drives them in the moment.

**Rule:** When a stated reason and an observed behavior don't match, trust the behavior and look for the System 1 driver.

## The shortlist (most useful in audience work)

### Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky)
People feel losses ~2x as strongly as equivalent gains. Status quo = familiar = default safe choice.
- *Surface behavior:* "I'm happy where I am."
- *Actual driver:* Switching feels like loss of control, identity, or social standing.
- *Creative implication:* Frame the new thing as *protection* of what they already have, not addition of something new.

### Status quo bias
People stay put unless pushed. Inertia is the default.
- *Surface behavior:* Endless deliberation, "I'll decide later."
- *Actual driver:* Deciding is costly; not-deciding feels safe.
- *Creative implication:* Lower the cost of the first step. Remove friction. Give permission.

### Social proof
When uncertain, people copy others — especially others like them.
- *Surface behavior:* "My friend recommended it."
- *Actual driver:* Uncertainty + desire for social validation.
- *Creative implication:* Show people like them making the same choice. Peer testimonials beat celebrity endorsement in high-stakes categories.

### Signaling (Veblen, Spence, Sutherland)
What people buy sends a signal to themselves and others about who they are.
- *Surface behavior:* Paying more for a visible brand.
- *Actual driver:* Identity construction; membership in a group.
- *Creative implication:* Product-as-badge. Who does this brand say you are?

### Loss of autonomy / reactance
When people feel their freedom is threatened, they push back — even against their own interest.
- *Surface behavior:* Rejecting advice from family, doctor, salesperson.
- *Actual driver:* Protecting agency and identity.
- *Creative implication:* Give them the choice. Don't tell them what they need. Let them arrive.

### Peak-end rule
Experiences are remembered by their most intense moment and their end, not their average.
- *Surface behavior:* A whole trip remembered for one meal or one tantrum.
- *Actual driver:* Memory compression.
- *Creative implication:* Engineer peak moments and strong endings in product/service design.

### Availability heuristic
People judge probability by what comes to mind easily. Vivid recent stories beat statistics.
- *Surface behavior:* Overestimating rare risks ("planes crash"), underestimating common ones.
- *Actual driver:* Cognitive ease.
- *Creative implication:* Make the desired story vivid and easy to recall.

### Anchoring
First number / frame seen biases all subsequent judgment.
- *Creative implication:* Control the opening frame. The first price, first competitor, first claim anchors everything after.

### Endowment effect
People value what they already own more than what they don't.
- *Creative implication:* Free trials, test drives, refund guarantees. Once it feels like theirs, they won't give it back.

### Choice architecture / defaults (Thaler)
People take the default option far more than they "should." Friction kills; smoothness wins.
- *Creative implication:* What's the default in the journey, and is it working for the brand or against it?

### Identity effects (Akerlof & Kranton)
People act in accordance with "people like me." Identity trumps utility.
- *Creative implication:* Who does this audience think they *are*? What does "someone like me" do in this category?

### Narrative / coherence
People need a story that makes their choice make sense. Facts that don't fit the story get ignored.
- *Creative implication:* Give them a story they can tell themselves (and others) about why this choice is right.

## Sutherland's psycho-logic (always worth asking)

- **What's the reverse of conventional wisdom here?** — the best answer is often the opposite of what the category assumes.
- **Where is a small, irrational-looking detail doing big emotional work?** — the free mint, the handwritten note, the weight of the glass.
- **What are they really buying?** — people don't buy drills, they buy holes; but often they're actually buying the feeling of being a capable homeowner.

## Output discipline

When you use a lens, say *which* lens you're using. Don't just write "they feel anxious" — write "**[Inferred]** Loss aversion: the move feels like losing their home, not gaining a new one."
