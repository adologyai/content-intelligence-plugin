# The voice dimensions — plotting a brand's tone

Use this file in step 4 (the spectrum plot) and step 5 (current vs. aspirational).

The eight dimensions below are designed to translate the chosen archetype personality into observable, testable voice behaviors. Each dimension is a continuum, not a binary. A brand sits *somewhere* on each — never fully at one end — and that position should be defendable in one sentence tied to the archetype.

For each dimension, you'll find:
- **What it measures** — what the dimension is actually about
- **Anchors** — what each end looks like, with brand examples
- **Position cues** — concrete verbal behaviors that mark different positions on the continuum
- **This, not that** — short before/after to make the position concrete
- **Common archetype mapping** — which archetypes typically sit where (a starting point, not a rule)

When you plot a brand, give each dimension a percentage (0–100, with 50 being neutral) and a one-sentence rationale. Then translate to a verbal cue a writer can act on.

---

## Dimension 1 — Funny ↔ Serious

**What it measures:** How much humour, wit, or levity the brand allows itself.

**Anchors:**
- **Full funny (0–20% serious):** Oatly, Liquid Death, Innocent Drinks, Cards Against Humanity
- **Witty serious (30–50%):** The Economist, Patagonia, Allbirds — humour in flashes, not constant
- **Mostly serious (60–80%):** Apple, Volvo, Bloomberg
- **Fully serious (80–100%):** Rolls-Royce, Mayo Clinic, Bloomberg Terminal

**Position cues:**
- *Funny end:* puns, self-deprecation, comic timing, breaking expectation, surreal asides
- *Serious end:* declarative claims, restrained vocabulary, no jokes even in headers

**This, not that:**
- *Funny brand:* "Plant milk that doesn't suck. We checked." NOT "Premium plant-based beverage made with care."
- *Serious brand:* "Considered, deliberate, unmistakable." NOT "We're the best — and yeah, we're not afraid to say it."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Funny end: Jester, Outlaw, Everyperson (when grounded in honest humour)
- Serious end: Ruler, Sage, Caregiver (in clinical contexts), Hero (in performance contexts)

---

## Dimension 2 — Casual ↔ Formal

**What it measures:** Register. How close to spoken speech the writing sits.

**Anchors:**
- **Fully casual (0–20% formal):** Glossier, Mailchimp, Innocent, most DTC brands
- **Conversational (30–50%):** Apple, IKEA, Airbnb
- **Considered formal (60–80%):** The New York Times, J.P. Morgan
- **Fully formal (80–100%):** Tiffany & Co., Hermès, traditional law firms

**Position cues:**
- *Casual end:* contractions, fragments, "yeah", "honestly", first-person, second-person direct address
- *Formal end:* complete sentences, no contractions, third-person where possible, "one", "the customer"

**This, not that:**
- *Casual brand:* "Yeah, we know — another email. But this one's actually useful." NOT "We are pleased to share important information with our valued customers."
- *Formal brand:* "Each piece is hand-finished in our Geneva workshop." NOT "We finish every piece by hand in Geneva — pretty cool, right?"

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Casual end: Jester, Outlaw, Everyperson, Caregiver (in friendly contexts)
- Formal end: Ruler, Sage (in some contexts), Lover (in editorial contexts)

---

## Dimension 3 — Irreverent ↔ Respectful

**What it measures:** Willingness to break category conventions, name enemies, or transgress norms.

**Anchors:**
- **Fully irreverent (0–20% respectful):** Liquid Death, Brewdog (peak era), Wendy's social, Cards Against Humanity
- **Sharp but not transgressive (30–50%):** Oatly, Patagonia (in their activist voice), Dollar Shave Club
- **Considered + polite (60–80%):** Apple, IKEA, Nike
- **Fully respectful (80–100%):** Most healthcare brands, Tiffany & Co., Pampers

**Position cues:**
- *Irreverent end:* willing to name competitors and enemies, occasional profanity, comfortable with controversy, breaks category visual/verbal codes deliberately
- *Respectful end:* never punches down, avoids inflammatory language, follows category conventions

**This, not that:**
- *Irreverent brand:* "The water industry is bullshit. Most water tastes like nothing and costs the earth." NOT "We're proud to offer a refreshing alternative."
- *Respectful brand:* "Compare our approach and decide what's right for you." NOT "Other agents are scammers. We're not."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Irreverent end: Outlaw, Jester (when sharp), Hero (when contrarian)
- Respectful end: Ruler, Caregiver, Sage, Innocent

**Note:** This is different from Casual ↔ Formal. A brand can be formal and irreverent (think Tom Ford's slightly raised eyebrow) or casual and respectful (think Mailchimp).

## Dimension 4 — Enthusiastic ↔ Matter-of-fact

**What it measures:** Emotional energy. How excited the brand allows itself to sound.

**Anchors:**
- **Full enthusiasm (0–20% matter-of-fact):** Trader Joe's, Mailchimp, early Innocent
- **Warm but measured (30–50%):** Apple, Nike, Patagonia
- **Restrained (60–80%):** Bloomberg, The Economist, Volvo
- **Pure matter-of-fact (80–100%):** Muji, technical/B2B brands, government services

**Position cues:**
- *Enthusiasm end:* exclamation marks (sparingly), positive intensifiers, expression of delight, comfortable with "love" and "amazing"
- *Matter-of-fact end:* declarative, no intensifiers, lets the facts do the work, comfortable with silence

**This, not that:**
- *Enthusiastic brand:* "We seriously love it when this happens. Welcome aboard." NOT "Account created. Please refer to documentation."
- *Matter-of-fact brand:* "Account created. Documentation in your inbox." NOT "OMG you're in! We're SO excited to have you!"

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Enthusiastic end: Lover, Innocent, Caregiver (warmer), Jester
- Matter-of-fact end: Sage, Ruler, Explorer (often stoic), Creator (often process-focused)

---

## Dimension 5 — Poetic ↔ Plain

**What it measures:** How much the writing reaches for figurative language, rhythm, and beauty.

**Anchors:**
- **Fully poetic (0–20% plain):** Aesop, Hermès, Apple in launch moments, Nike's hero films
- **Crafted but clear (30–50%):** Patagonia, The New Yorker
- **Plain with rhythm (60–80%):** IKEA, Muji
- **Pure plain (80–100%):** Government services, B2B utility brands, Aldi

**Position cues:**
- *Poetic end:* metaphor, rhythm, deliberate ambiguity, vivid imagery, willing to be slightly oblique
- *Plain end:* one idea per sentence, no metaphor, says what it means

**This, not that:**
- *Poetic brand:* "The weight of a thing well-made." NOT "Heavy because it's high quality."
- *Plain brand:* "Heavy because the steel is thick." NOT "Built with a weight that speaks of care."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Poetic end: Lover, Creator, Magician, Sage (in editorial contexts)
- Plain end: Everyperson, Innocent (factual variety), Caregiver (clarity-first contexts), Hero (action over flourish)

---

## Dimension 6 — Provocative ↔ Reserved

**What it measures:** Willingness to make pointed claims, take sides, or stake territory.

**Anchors:**
- **Fully provocative (0–20% reserved):** Brewdog, Liquid Death, Patagonia ("Don't buy this jacket")
- **Pointed (30–50%):** Apple, Nike, Tesla in early voice
- **Considered (60–80%):** Most premium brands, healthcare
- **Fully reserved (80–100%):** Tiffany, traditional luxury, government communications

**Position cues:**
- *Provocative end:* takes a side, names what they're against, makes counter-conventional claims
- *Reserved end:* lets the work speak, avoids commentary, no enemies

**This, not that:**
- *Provocative brand:* "Most running shoes are designed for a foot strike that doesn't exist." NOT "Our shoes use innovative design principles."
- *Reserved brand:* "Each pair benefits from forty years of running expertise." NOT "Other brands have it wrong. We have it right."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Provocative end: Outlaw, Hero, Magician (in transformative claims)
- Reserved end: Ruler, Lover (in luxury contexts), Sage (in measured contexts)

---

## Dimension 7 — Confident ↔ Modest

**What it measures:** How much the brand asserts its own quality, capability, or position.

**Anchors:**
- **Full confidence (0–20% modest):** Apple, Tesla, Tiffany, most Ruler brands
- **Earned confidence (30–50%):** Patagonia, Nike, Sage brands
- **Quietly confident (60–80%):** Muji, some Innocent brands, craft brands
- **Active modesty (80–100%):** Some Everyperson brands, "no big deal" positioning

**Position cues:**
- *Confidence end:* direct claims, no hedging, comfortable with superlatives where earned, says "the" not "a"
- *Modesty end:* understates, lets others draw conclusions, comfortable with "we tried", "it turned out"

**This, not that:**
- *Confident brand:* "The best laptop we've ever built." NOT "We think you'll really like our newest model."
- *Modest brand:* "Took us a while to get it right. We think this version is." NOT "The most revolutionary product in the category."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Confident end: Ruler, Hero, Magician, Outlaw (in their own terms)
- Modest end: Everyperson, Innocent, Caregiver (deflecting attention), Creator (in process-talk)

---

## Dimension 8 — Distinctive ↔ Conventional

**What it measures:** How far the brand deviates from category norms in word choice, rhythm, and structure.

**Anchors:**
- **Fully distinctive (0–20% conventional):** Liquid Death, Oatly, Aesop, Patagonia
- **Distinctively branded (30–50%):** Apple, Nike, Glossier
- **Recognizably category-fluent (60–80%):** Most challenger brands once they scale
- **Fully conventional (80–100%):** Generic category players, white-label competitors

**Position cues:**
- *Distinctive end:* unusual word choices, idiosyncratic rhythm, breaks category visual/verbal conventions, has a recognizable "house style"
- *Conventional end:* uses category-standard vocabulary ("premium", "innovative", "quality"), familiar structures, indistinguishable from competitors in blind tests

**This, not that:**
- *Distinctive brand:* "Beer for plant murderers." NOT "Premium craft beer with a unique flavor profile."
- *Conventional brand:* (if intentionally so for a value play) "Good beer. Fair price." NOT "Disrupting the category with bold flavor choices."

**Common archetype mapping:**
- Distinctive end: Outlaw, Jester, Creator, Magician
- Conventional end: most category-leader Rulers (who *own* the conventional language), Everyperson (deliberate plainness)

**Important:** Distinctiveness is not always the goal. A category-leading Ruler can win by *owning* the conventional language so completely nobody else can use it. But a challenger brand at conventional end of this dimension is usually invisible.

## How to use this file in step 4 (the spectrum plot)

For the brand you're plotting:

1. Read the archetype personality from step 2.
2. Go through each dimension. For each, decide where the archetype implies the brand should sit. Use the **Common archetype mapping** as a starting point — but make a judgement; a Caregiver brand can absolutely be 70% distinctive (Burt's Bees) or 30% distinctive (most healthcare).
3. Give each dimension a position (percentage) and one sentence of rationale tied to the archetype.
4. Write a verbal cue — the rule a writer can apply. E.g., "Contractions yes. Slang occasionally. Profanity never."

**Output format:**

```
Casual ↔ Formal: 75% Casual
  Rationale: Caregiver primary, with Jester secondary — warmth needs an unguarded, spoken register, but the brand keeps it grammatically clean.
  Verbal cue: Contractions throughout. Fragments when they land. First-person plural ("we") and direct "you". Avoid "one" and "our valued customers".
```

## How to use this file in step 5 (current vs. aspirational)

Use the same 8 dimensions. Plot two positions per dimension:
- **Today** — based on the Adology audit. Read the actual copy and decide where the brand currently sits.
- **Target** — based on the chosen personality from step 2.

For each dimension, calculate the gap. Three buckets:
- **Significant gap (>25 percentage points):** Flag for user discussion. The team will feel this change.
- **Moderate gap (10–25 points):** Note it. Likely achievable with conscious writing.
- **Minor gap (<10 points):** Already close. No special attention needed.

For significant gaps, present the choice to the user: aim for the full target, or aim for a bridging position closer to where the brand sits today? This is a change-management call as much as a brand call.
