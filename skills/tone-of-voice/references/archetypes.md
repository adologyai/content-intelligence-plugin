# The 12 Jungian Brand Archetypes — voice reference

Use this file as the classification rubric in step 1 (archetype audit) and as the menu for step 2 (assigning intended personality).

Each archetype below has:
- **Core motivation** — what the archetype is fundamentally trying to do
- **Audience role** — what relationship the archetype offers the audience
- **Voice markers** — observable patterns in word choice, sentence rhythm, emotional register
- **Vocabulary cues** — typical words and phrases (use these for classification, not just for inspiration)
- **Sample phrasing** — the kind of sentence this archetype writes
- **Brand examples** — real-world reference points
- **Common pairings** — secondary archetypes that combine well with this one as primary
- **Watch-outs** — failure modes and category-overcrowded zones

The 12 archetypes split into four motivational families:

1. **Stability / Order** — Ruler, Caregiver, Creator
2. **Belonging / Enjoyment** — Lover, Jester, Everyperson
3. **Risk / Mastery** — Hero, Outlaw, Magician
4. **Independence / Spirit** — Innocent, Sage, Explorer

A strong brand owns one primary archetype and pairs it with 1–2 secondaries — usually from a *different* family (combining within a family tends to flatten rather than add dimension).

---

## 1. The Ruler

**Core motivation:** Control, order, prosperity. Wants the world to run well and to be seen running it.

**Audience role:** Makes the audience feel they're in safe, capable, premium hands.

**Voice markers:**
- Declarative, complete sentences
- Refined, restrained vocabulary
- Authoritative without shouting
- Long-form rhythm; rarely uses contractions
- Implicit hierarchy ("the standard", "the original", "the definitive")

**Vocabulary cues:** *standard, excellence, heritage, mastery, refined, the* (as a possessive emphasis), *legacy, distinction, considered, definitive*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "The standard against which others are measured."
- "Crafted in the same workshop since 1837."
- "Considered, deliberate, unmistakable."

**Brand examples:** Rolex, Mercedes-Benz, American Express, The Economist, Hermès

**Common pairings:** Sage (authority + wisdom), Creator (authority + craft), Caregiver (authority + service)

**Watch-outs:**
- Easily reads as cold, exclusionary, or smug.
- Category-crowded in luxury, finance, and premium auto — distinctiveness comes from the secondary.
- Avoid the contemporary trap of "premium" being signalled only with negative space and serif fonts; the voice has to do real work.

---

## 2. The Caregiver

**Core motivation:** To protect and care for others. Selfless, generous, nurturing.

**Audience role:** Makes the audience feel looked after, seen, taken seriously without being patronised.

**Voice markers:**
- Warm but not saccharine
- "You" and "we" heavily used; "us" implies relationship
- Gentle directness; permission-giving language
- Reassurance without minimising
- Reads like a thoughtful family member or a trusted nurse

**Vocabulary cues:** *care, here for you, we'll handle, no judgment, take your time, looked after, we've got you, gentle, supportive, ready when you are*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Whenever you're ready, we'll be here."
- "We'll take care of the hard parts."
- "No pressure. No deadline. Just the help you need."

**Brand examples:** Johnson & Johnson, Volvo, Dove, Campbell's, Pampers

**Common pairings:** Sage (caring expertise), Innocent (caring optimism), Ruler (caring authority — think family doctor or premium healthcare)

**Watch-outs:**
- Slips into condescension or pity if "you" becomes "you poor thing."
- Category-crowded in healthcare, baby, and senior care — sharpen with a secondary.
- Mission alignment matters: a brand that doesn't actually take care of its customers (e.g., big-box retailers with brutal service) will be called out for performative Caregiver voice.

---

## 3. The Creator

**Core motivation:** To make something new, lasting, original. Imagination as a way of being.

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as fellow makers — people with taste, ambition, and the ability to bring something into being.

**Voice markers:**
- Specific, sensory, often visual language
- Attention to craft and process; named materials, named techniques
- Confident in idiosyncrasy
- Mixes high and low register
- Rarely uses superlatives; prefers concrete specifics

**Vocabulary cues:** *make, build, design, original, hand, idea, sketch, draft, prototype, version, the way we, our process, we wanted*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "We made this because we wanted one for ourselves."
- "Eighteen months of prototypes. Three that we kept."
- "Cut from a single piece of brass. No screws."

**Brand examples:** Apple (peak Creator era), LEGO, Adobe, Patagonia (Creator + Hero), Lululemon's design language, Hermès in their craft storytelling

**Common pairings:** Ruler (master craftsperson), Magician (creative + visionary), Outlaw (creator-rebel — think Off-White or Glossier early days)

**Watch-outs:**
- Can become self-indulgent ("we obsessed for months over the perfect…") without earning it.
- Process-talk only works if the process is genuinely distinctive.
- Often slips into "design language" generic — sans-serif, "crafted with care," white space — without the voice doing real differentiation.

---

## 4. The Lover

**Core motivation:** Intimacy, sensual pleasure, connection. Wants to be wanted and to make others feel wanted.

**Audience role:** Invites the audience into something pleasurable, beautiful, or intimate.

**Voice markers:**
- Sensory and embodied — taste, touch, smell, sound, warmth
- Slower rhythm; longer vowels; less syntactic urgency
- Direct address as a kind of seduction ("for you", "you'll find")
- Comfortable with desire as a motivator (without being crass)
- Tonally close to fashion editorial when done well

**Vocabulary cues:** *indulge, devote, melt, linger, savour, touch, warm, soft, deep, you, yours, between us*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Take your time. Or don't."
- "The kind of warmth that stays."
- "Made to be touched."

**Brand examples:** Victoria's Secret (peak Lover), Chanel, Godiva, Häagen-Dazs, Aesop, Diptyque

**Common pairings:** Creator (sensual + crafted), Ruler (sensual + refined), Innocent (sensual + pure — think bridal)

**Watch-outs:**
- Easily veers into cliché ("indulgent," "luxurious," "decadent") if it doesn't have the brand world to back it up.
- Category-crowded in beauty, fragrance, chocolate, lingerie — distinctiveness has to come from the *specific* sensuality (Aesop's intellectual sensuality is different from Victoria's Secret's overt sensuality).
- Tone-deaf in B2B and most utilitarian categories.

---

## 5. The Jester

**Core motivation:** To have a good time and to make the audience have one too. Lives in the present.

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as in on the joke — equals, not the butt of it.

**Voice markers:**
- Short, punchy sentences
- Comic timing — setup and payoff structure
- Willing to break rules of grammar, formality, or convention
- Self-aware without being smug
- Specific is funnier than general

**Vocabulary cues:** *anyway, yeah no, look, honestly, the thing is, fine, sure, lol-adjacent informality, generous use of contractions and fragments*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "You know what's wild? This actually works."
- "Look, we tried. It went weirdly well."
- "Three pockets. Why three? Reasons."

**Brand examples:** Oatly, Old Spice, Innocent Drinks, Cards Against Humanity, Liquid Death, Wendy's (peak social media era)

**Common pairings:** Outlaw (rebellious + funny), Everyperson (relatable + funny), Sage (clever + funny — think the New Yorker's voice)

**Watch-outs:**
- Hardest archetype to do well — bad Jester voice reads as try-hard or "How do you do, fellow kids."
- Requires a real comic sensibility behind the writing; not every team has one.
- Doesn't travel as well across markets — humour is the most culturally local register.
- Service voice has to dial *way* back; nobody wants a joke when their order's late.

---

## 6. The Everyperson

**Core motivation:** Belonging, connection, equality. Just wants to be part of things.

**Audience role:** "We're like you." No pretence, no airs, no special status.

**Voice markers:**
- Plain language, everyday vocabulary
- Sentence rhythm matches spoken speech
- Self-deprecating where helpful; never grandiose
- Familiar references — supermarkets, kitchens, commutes, kids
- Honest about limitations

**Vocabulary cues:** *real, regular, everyday, like the rest of us, honest, decent, fair, normal, no fuss, no nonsense, simple*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "We just wanted to make a good one and not charge a fortune for it."
- "Honestly, it's just toothpaste. But it's a really good one."
- "Same coffee, lower price. That's it."

**Brand examples:** IKEA, Aldi (in some markets), Costco, Target, Greggs (UK), Walmart's "Everyday Low Prices" era

**Common pairings:** Caregiver (honest + caring), Jester (honest + funny — peak Aldi UK), Sage (honest + smart)

**Watch-outs:**
- Easy to write generically — every brand that says "regular people" sounds the same.
- Real Everyperson voice is grounded in specifics ("the queue at school pickup", "Tuesday night, knackered") not abstractions ("everyday moments").
- Wrong fit for premium and aspirational categories — Everyperson signals "fine" not "best."

---

## 7. The Hero

**Core motivation:** To prove worth through courage, achievement, mastery. Improvement, effort, victory.

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as capable of more — sees their potential and calls them to it.

**Voice markers:**
- Direct, second-person, imperative-leaning
- Short, declarative sentences
- Action verbs over abstract nouns
- Urgency without panic
- Comfortable with the language of effort, difficulty, and reward

**Vocabulary cues:** *push, train, earn, master, achieve, beat, build, go, run, climb, you can, just, no excuses*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Show up. That's the whole thing."
- "The miles aren't going to run themselves."
- "Built for the hard days."

**Brand examples:** Nike (the archetypal Hero), Under Armour, GoPro, Gatorade, Strava

**Common pairings:** Outlaw (Hero who breaks rules), Magician (Hero who transforms), Sage (Hero who teaches — think Peloton instructors at their best)

**Watch-outs:**
- Massively crowded in fitness, sports, and performance categories.
- Easy to read as bullying or shame-inducing ("no excuses!") if not grounded in genuine care.
- The line between Hero and Caregiver matters — Hero pushes; Caregiver supports. Mixing them sloppily creates whiplash.

---

## 8. The Outlaw

**Core motivation:** Disruption, freedom, breaking what doesn't work. Defines self against the establishment.

**Audience role:** Invites the audience into the resistance. Treats them as people who see through the system.

**Voice markers:**
- Direct, sometimes provocative; comfortable with edge
- Willing to name enemies (the establishment, the competitor, the bullshit)
- Profanity, slang, or transgressive language used deliberately
- Short, punchy rhythm; comfortable with one-word sentences
- Confidence verging on swagger

**Vocabulary cues:** *fuck (where the brand allows), screw, break, ditch, who decided, why is this still, what if instead, the truth is, the real, no*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Most water tastes like nothing. Ours tastes like death."
- "Sliced bread? We're doing better than that."
- "The category said no. We said try us."

**Brand examples:** Harley-Davidson, Diesel, Liquid Death, Brewdog (peak Outlaw era), Patagonia (Outlaw + Hero hybrid), early Dollar Shave Club

**Common pairings:** Jester (Outlaw who's funny), Hero (rebel hero), Creator (rule-breaking maker)

**Watch-outs:**
- Wears thin fast if the brand isn't actually challenging anything material.
- Performative Outlaw voice (lots of "fuck the system" without action) gets called out.
- Service voice needs to be human and respectful even when marketing voice is provocative; an Outlaw brand whose CS team is rude is just rude.

---

## 9. The Magician

**Core motivation:** To make dreams come true. Transformation, vision, "what if it could be different."

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as people on the cusp of a transformation — they're about to become.

**Voice markers:**
- Promises change but anchors it in mechanism (otherwise it's just hand-waving)
- Visionary register without becoming abstract
- Lots of "before and after" framing
- Comfortable with the language of possibility, intention, ritual
- Mystery is fine, but not vagueness

**Vocabulary cues:** *transform, unlock, awaken, reveal, become, before / after, what if, imagine, the moment, the shift*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "The version of you who took the holiday is the version you'll keep."
- "Five days. The shift is real."
- "Before this, work felt like work."

**Brand examples:** Disney, Tesla (early), Apple (in product launch voice), Airbnb, Peloton (in their transformation narrative)

**Common pairings:** Creator (transformative + crafted), Sage (transformative + wise), Hero (transformative + striving)

**Watch-outs:**
- Easy to slip into hollow promises if the product can't deliver the transformation.
- Becomes empty quickly in wellness and self-improvement categories where every brand says "transform your life."
- The discipline is: anchor every "transformation" claim in a concrete mechanism.

---

## 10. The Innocent

**Core motivation:** Purity, simplicity, optimism, being good. Faith that things can be simple and good.

**Audience role:** Invites the audience into a simpler, kinder version of the world.

**Voice markers:**
- Plain, unadorned vocabulary
- Sentence structures a child could read
- Optimistic without being naive
- Often nature-, light-, or origin-focused
- Quiet rather than loud

**Vocabulary cues:** *simple, pure, real, good, fresh, clean, just, only, one thing, the basics, original*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Three ingredients. Nothing else."
- "Just good bread."
- "The way it was meant to be."

**Brand examples:** Innocent Drinks (named for it), Method, Burt's Bees, Mrs. Meyer's, Aesop's plainer registers, Coca-Cola's classic positioning

**Common pairings:** Caregiver (simple + caring), Sage (simple + wise), Everyperson (simple + grounded)

**Watch-outs:**
- Often confused with Everyperson — the difference is that Innocent is *aspirationally* simple (faith that life *can be* simple), where Everyperson is *factually* ordinary.
- Category-crowded in natural/organic — distinctiveness comes from a specific kind of simplicity, not a vague "natural" claim.
- Naive Innocent voice in serious categories (finance, healthcare) can feel dismissive of complexity the customer actually faces.

---

## 11. The Sage

**Core motivation:** Truth, understanding, wisdom. Wants to know — and wants others to know.

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as intelligent adults who can handle nuance, complexity, and being told what's actually true.

**Voice markers:**
- Measured, precise, well-constructed sentences
- Distinguishes carefully between what is known, suspected, and unknown
- Uses qualifying language thoughtfully ("often", "tends to", "in our experience")
- Rare use of superlatives; comfortable with "it depends"
- Confident in expertise without performing it

**Vocabulary cues:** *consider, suggest, evidence, learn, understand, the research, in practice, the question is, it depends, why*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Most diets fail. Here's what the data actually shows."
- "The honest answer is: it depends on three things."
- "Considered, but not rushed."

**Brand examples:** The New York Times, Bloomberg, McKinsey (in their public voice), Headspace (in psychoeducation), The Economist, Wired

**Common pairings:** Ruler (authoritative + wise), Caregiver (wise + caring — think trusted doctor), Magician (wise + transformative)

**Watch-outs:**
- Often confused with Ruler — the difference is that Sage wants to *share* the truth where Ruler wants to *embody* the standard.
- Category-crowded in education, finance, and consulting — distinctiveness comes from how the wisdom is *delivered* (warm, dry, witty, plain) not from claiming expertise.
- Risk of being read as cold or distant unless paired with warmth.

---

## 12. The Explorer

**Core motivation:** Freedom, discovery, the journey. Restless; finds meaning in moving, in newness, in the road.

**Audience role:** Treats the audience as fellow travellers — people who can't stay put, who are looking for something.

**Voice markers:**
- Outward-focused; lots of place, motion, landscape
- Comfortable with the unknown and unfinished
- Direct, often spare; doesn't oversell
- First-person plural ("we're going", "we found")
- Specific places, routes, conditions — concrete not abstract

**Vocabulary cues:** *out there, on the road, find, head, take, the route, the way, no map, open, ahead, off, beyond*

**Sample phrasing:**
- "Built for the way out, not the way home."
- "If the road exists, this'll get you down it."
- "Three days in. Forty more to go."

**Brand examples:** Patagonia (Explorer + Hero), The North Face, REI, Land Rover, Subaru, Jeep, Airbnb (in their early "live there" era), Howler Brothers

**Common pairings:** Hero (explorer + striver), Outlaw (independent + transgressive), Sage (wise + curious)

**Watch-outs:**
- Crowded in outdoor and adventure categories — distinctiveness comes from the *kind* of exploration (Patagonia's environmentalism vs. Jeep's classic Americana).
- "Adventure-coded" language can become parody fast if there's no real adventure being offered.
- Doesn't travel well into urban / convenience categories without significant translation.

## How to use this file in step 1 (the audit)

For each Adology item you classify:

1. Read the actual copy, headline, transcript, on-screen text.
2. Note the dominant emotional register and verbal pattern.
3. Match against the **voice markers** and **vocabulary cues** lists above — these are diagnostic.
4. Assign a primary archetype for that item. If two archetypes feel almost equally present, note both.
5. Be consistent across the sample — if you're calling something "Hero", the next item that fits the same pattern should also be called "Hero."

When you tally, surface the top 6. Anything below the top 6 is usually noise (one-off content choices, agency variation).

## How to use this file in step 2 (assigning intended personality)

For each archetype you're considering as the brand's primary or secondary:

1. Re-read its **Core motivation** and ask: does this align with the brand's mission and values from the website? If not, rule it out.
2. Re-read **Brand examples** and ask: does the user want to live in this neighbourhood? Is there a competitor in their actual category from this list?
3. Re-read **Common pairings** to think through the secondary options.
4. Re-read **Watch-outs** to anticipate where the user will push back or where execution will fail.

When you present the proposed personality to the user, lean on the archetype's **audience role** and **brings** language to make the rationale concrete — not just "we're going Hero" but "Hero means we treat customers as capable of more, calling them to growth rather than babying them. That fits your mission of [X] and pushes against [competitor]'s caregiver register."
